The Co.LLab™ · The operating model

Omnidirectional Enterprise™ is an economic enterprise system.

Omnidirectional Enterprise™ is an economic enterprise system that connects people, organizations, products, intellectual property, capital, and resources in one ecosystem. It is the operating model of The Co.LLab™.

How the system works

Generating value and capturing value are different processes.

Economic performance depends not only on creating value, but on the systems governing how resources are coordinated, intellectual property is owned, decisions are made, innovations reach markets, and returns are captured and reinvested.

Omnidirectional Enterprise™ is organized as nine branches, each a place where economic activity happens: Businesses, Nonprofits, Communities, People, Products & Services, Technology, Capital, Knowledge + IP, and Creators. The IV Phases™ (Foundations, Planning, Operating, and Preservation) give every branch one common way to decide and organize.

Research and economic foundations

The published ideas the model builds on.

These are independent, published works in economics and management. They explain why coordination, governance, intellectual property and ecosystems matter to economic outcomes, the same questions Omnidirectional Enterprise™ is built around. The summaries are our own plain-language readings. The authors and publishers have not endorsed The Co.LLab™, and the works are not evidence that the model produces any particular result.

Ronald Coase

The Nature of the Firm

Economica, 1937

Organizations exist because using markets has costs. When coordinating inside an organization is cheaper than contracting for each piece, the organization is the better way to coordinate resources.

Oliver Williamson

The Economic Institutions of Capitalism

Free Press, 1985

How an activity is governed affects its economic result. Shared rules and clear decision rights lower the cost of working together. Williamson shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for this line of work.

Elinor Ostrom

Governing the Commons

Cambridge University Press, 1990

Shared resources can be managed well by the people who use them, when rules, roles and monitoring are clear and the rules fit local conditions. Ostrom shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics.

David Teece

Profiting from Innovation

Research Policy, 1986

Creating an innovation and capturing its returns are different problems. Who profits depends on ownership of the idea and on the complementary assets needed to bring it to market.

Paul Romer

Endogenous Technological Change

Journal of Political Economy, 1990

Ideas, unlike physical goods, can be used by many people at once, and investment in new ideas drives long-run growth. Romer received the 2018 Nobel Prize in Economics for work on this.

Jonathan Haskel and Stian Westlake

Capitalism without Capital

Princeton University Press, 2017

Investment in intangible assets such as intellectual property, software, design and organizational know-how is a large and growing part of how modern economies create value, and intangibles behave differently from physical assets.

James Moore

Predators and Prey: A New Ecology of Competition

Harvard Business Review, 1993

Companies can be understood as members of business ecosystems, where participants from different sectors coevolve around shared capabilities rather than competing as isolated firms.
